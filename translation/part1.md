**Preface**

The god Kanjin, who so loved the plum and the pine, wrote in one of his poems that even people who live a thousand ri apart, with sea and mountain between them, can talk together through the woven threads of writing — and how true that is! Such is the virtue of a flourishing age of letters: sitting at home, we may learn all about the state of things in lands ten thousand ri across the sea, and lying in bed we may look into the peace and the troubles of all the foreign nations. This is the blessing of the Great Peace, for which we should be humbly grateful.

And yet the strange scripts of China and Holland, and the sounds of foreign words, are not easy reading for our children and our women. So I have picked out, from many books, scenes of lands ten thousand ri away, and rendered them gently into the kana of our own country. I have called the result *Tales of the Myriad Countries*; think of it as nothing more than a book of bedside stories for those grown weary of the long spring days and the long autumn nights.

*Spring of Man'en 2, the year of the Metal Cock (1861). Written by Kanagaki Robun.*

[T.N. Kanjin is the deified courtier-poet Sugawara no Michizane (845–903), the patron god of learning, famous for his love of the plum tree. A ri is about 3.9 km.]

**Captions:** "Russia" / "England"

**Russia.** This country straddles the continents of Europe and Asia, and its capital is called Petersburg. Areki, the founding emperor of its revival, was outstanding in talent and virtue; he greatly enlarged his dominions and held the imperial throne. After he died, his consort Catherine took the throne as empress and governed, and from then on, it is said, the country grew more and more enlightened.

**England.** This country is a great island nation in the sea to the west of Europe. At its revival its dominions spread far and wide: in the reign of Queen Anne it joined to itself the islands of Scotland and Ireland, and the whole is called Great Britain. Its capital is London, on the river Thames. The land is divided into fifty-two counties.

[T.N. "Areki" is printed as a separate small column of katakana beside "the founding emperor"; it is probably a name (Alexei?) followed by the topic particle. Since his consort Catherine succeeds him, the emperor meant is clearly Peter the Great, whose story is told below.]

Her might shook all Europe; the country grew rich and the people prospered; trade flourished, and at last she became the most famous ruler in all Europe. She then yielded the throne to the next prince, Peter the Third. This emperor too was a sovereign of wisdom, virtue and excellence: he carried on the work of his father and mother, guarded his people firmly, and moreover pacified the various countries, so that of all the kings of Europe there was not one who did not bow to Russia and obey her. That in these days she has become an empire equal to our own country — these were happy things indeed. And so all ended happily.

[T.N. Robun's history is confused here. The runaway prince was Alexei, Peter's son by his first wife, who fled abroad in 1716 and died in 1718 under questioning ordered by Peter himself, before Catherine's reign. Catherine I reigned only 1725–27 and was succeeded by Alexei's son, Peter II, not "Peter the Third".]
