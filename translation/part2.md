# Part Two (二編): America

*Picture Tales of the Myriad Countries* (童絵解万国噺), Part Two, in two volumes (upper and lower).
Text by Kanagaki Robun, pictures by Ichimōsai Yoshitora; published by Kinkyōdō, Edo, spring 1861.

Part Two covers North America and the United States: how the land was settled, the war with England and
independence, how the government is organised, and the land, its products and the customs of its people.

**Preface.** Long, long ago there was a little red-book story with a very simple plot, called *Asahina's Island Voyages*. It took him to the lands of the Long-Legs and the Long-Arms, and it whiled away many a long spring day for small children, who found it a fine plaything. There is also a society in the country called the Kankwakai, the "Society for Encouraging Peace". If its members quarrel, or if two countries are not on friendly terms, it urges them to make peace and binds them together like brothers.

○ When the land was first opened up there were no people in it yet. The Lord on High made one man and set him over all the people as their master. Then he made a woman and gave her to him as his wife. That was the beginning of marriage, and it has come down from generation to generation without changing.

In this country nobody keeps concubines at all. There is never any case of a boy and girl being promised in marriage while they are still children. When a young man is fifteen or older, he looks for a good girl. If she is not a close relative, he tells her father and elder brother and they become friends. They visit back and forth for three to five years, until each side knows the other's nature and conduct well. Or else the two meet face to face and settle the wedding day themselves, and each of their parents [...]. There are none of the customs of betrothal gifts and gift-lists. On the wedding day the man and woman go up into the hall and take each other's hands, *(continued)*

In the twenty-six states of the United States there is no such thing as lord and vassal, only a distinction between higher and lower. From the President down, through the officials of the nation, of the states, of the cities and of the counties, ranks differ. But there is no difference at all in their clothes or their food. Only when it comes to serving people in authority, everyone treats them with respect. The three bonds of parent and child, brothers and friends are much the same as in China.

They eat three meals a day [...] wheat [...] and tea [?] and so on. Besides these there are chicken, pork, duck, beef and mutton, mostly roasted, which they cut up and eat, along with fruit, dry sweets, cow's milk, eggs and the like. There is tea and there is wine. In each house they set up a single table, spread a cloth over it, and place the various dishes on it. They serve each person his food on a plate of his own, and use no chopsticks, only a slim little knife. When they go out they use carriages and horses.

There are no festival days in the year, and no day is lucky or unlucky. Only every seventh day they hold a service of worship. Apart from that, things are no different from China.

When someone dies, one person washes the body, dresses it in a single undergarment, folds the hands together in prayer, and lays it in a coffin. After three to five days it is buried. The grave is walled on four sides with stone, iron or tin, and then the coffin is laid in it. The close relatives who see it off wear black. A stone monument is set up on the grave, carved on its face with the dead person's birthday and the day of death, and a stone railing runs round all four sides, they say, to keep sheep from trampling it.
