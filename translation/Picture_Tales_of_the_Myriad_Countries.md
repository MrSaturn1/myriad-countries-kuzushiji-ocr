## About this book

Seven years after Perry's ships opened Japan, the popular writer Kanagaki Robun
set out to tell children "and women" about the foreign countries now arriving
at Japan's ports. He worked from Japanese and Chinese digests of Western
geography (his Part 3 preface names *Haiguo tuzhi* and other works), retold in
easy kana with a picture on every page.

The four parts translated here move from fact toward fable:

- **Part 1** surveys Russia, England, France, Holland and North America, and
  ends with a long, romantic life of Peter the Great.
- **Part 2** is a straightforward account of America: the colonies, the
  quarrel with England, the Declaration ("twelve articles" of grievances),
  independence, the presidency, and American schools, charities, marriage,
  food and funerals.
- **Part 3** starts with Columbus and Americus, then turns into a historical
  romance in the style of Japanese popular fiction. Meriken, an Englishman
  descended from Americus, and the lady-in-waiting Harina, descended from
  Columbus, are framed for breaking the Queen's jewel and exiled. A contrary
  demon blows their ship to Virginia, where their son George Washington is
  born and grows up to slay a poison-breathing mountain monster.
- **Part 4** continues the romance: Washington meets Franklin and his sister
  Caroline, and the dutiful young Adams kills a thousand-year-old serpent with
  the help of a mountain immortal and a white eagle, then trains village youths
  in Western arms against the day of rebellion. It ends "to be continued": Robun
  promises a Part 5 in which the three heroes swear brotherhood to free the
  thirteen states.

Robun's history is often wrong and sometimes invented. It has been translated as
he wrote it. Short translator's notes point out the larger departures where a
reader might otherwise be puzzled.

## How the text was made

The Japanese was transcribed from the scans first. Two OCR programs from the
National Diet Library (NDL古典籍OCR-Lite and the full NDL古典籍OCR ver.3) read
each page. Their readings were merged, and Claude Opus 5.5 checked every
disagreement against enlarged crops of the scans, using the second library
copy wherever the first was worn. On five test spreads scored against careful
reference transcriptions, this process got about 98% of characters right.
Either OCR program alone got 92–95%, and Claude reading the images with no
OCR got 67%. Claude Opus 5.5 then translated the corrected text. The
Japanese transcriptions are in `ocr/final/`, and the evaluation is in
`ocr/EVALUATION.md`.

## Conventions

- Each section is one opening of the book: two facing pages. The heading names
  the scan it comes from, so you can look at the picture while you read.
  Japanese books open from the right, and each opening is read right page first.
- **Captions** are the labels and titles printed in the pictures.
- **[?]** follows a word or phrase whose reading is uncertain.
- **[…]** marks text that can't be read in any copy.
- **[T.N. …]** is a translator's note.
- Names: real people and places are given their usual English spellings, and
  the forms Robun printed are listed in the Glossary. His invented characters
  (Meriken, Harina, Furore, Franboresu, Majisa, Ejise…) are romanized as printed.
- *ri* (里) ≈ 3.9 km; *chō* (丁) ≈ 109 m; *jō* (丈) ≈ 3 m.

**Captions:** "Picture Tales of the Myriad Countries, Explained for Children" / "Part One" / "Lower Volume" / "Translated by Kanagaki Robun" / "Pictures by Mōsai Yoshitora" / "Blocks held by Yamadaya"

**Preface**

The god Kanjin, who so loved the plum and the pine, wrote in one of his poems that even people who live a thousand ri apart, with sea and mountain between them, can talk together through the woven threads of writing — and how true that is! Such is the virtue of a flourishing age of letters: sitting at home, we may learn all about the state of things in lands ten thousand ri across the sea, and lying in bed we may look into the peace and the troubles of all the foreign nations. This is the blessing of the Great Peace, for which we should be humbly grateful.

And yet the strange scripts of China and Holland, and the sounds of foreign words, are not easy reading for our children and our women. So I have picked out, from many books, scenes of lands ten thousand ri away, and rendered them gently into the kana of our own country. I have called the result *Tales of the Myriad Countries*; think of it as nothing more than a book of bedside stories for those grown weary of the long spring days and the long autumn nights.

*Spring of Man'en 2, the year of the Metal Cock (1861). Written by Kanagaki Robun.*

[T.N. Kanjin is the deified courtier-poet Sugawara no Michizane (845–903), the patron god of learning, famous for his love of the plum tree. A ri is about 3.9 km.]

# Part Three (三編): Columbus, Meriken and Harina, and the Birth of Washington

*童絵解万国噺 三編*, text by Kanagaki Robun, pictures by Ichimōsai Yoshitora, published by Kinkyōdō, spring 1861.
Translated from the corrected transcriptions in `ocr/final/` (NDL copy, vol. 3, with one leaf and one picture page from the Waseda copy).

Part Three begins with Columbus's voyage and the naming of America after Americus. It then tells a tale of Robun's own: Meriken, an Englishman descended from Americus, and Harina, a lady-in-waiting descended from Columbus, are exiled to America. Their son is George Washington. The part ends with young Washington's fight against the monster of Mount Kakumaku and his meeting with Franklin.

# Appendix: pages found only in the Waseda copy

The Waseda University Library copy (文庫11 a0380) is a later issue of the book
from a different publisher, Yamadaya Shōjirō (山田屋庄次郎), rebound as two
volumes. Its story pages are the same as the NDL copy's (one leaf the NDL copy
lacks is translated in its place in Part 3), but it adds an endpaper and a
colophon of its own.

### Endpaper: the Six Jewel Rivers — waseda_vol1_p0002  (scan: 童絵解万国噺 (1861) - Waseda 文庫11 a0380/vol1/p0002.jpg)

Six fan-shaped panels, each carrying one of the classical poems on the "Six
Jewel Rivers" (六玉川, *Mu-Tamagawa*): six rivers named Tamagawa in different
provinces, a favourite subject of poets and print designers. The printed
versions differ in places from the standard texts of the poems.

**Yamashiro: the Jewel River of Ide**
> I'll halt my horse and water him once more —
> where dew from the yellow kerria blossoms
> drips into the Jewel River of Ide.

**Kii: the Jewel River of Kōya**
> Travellers, forgetting, may they not scoop and drink it —
> the water of the Jewel River
> deep in the mountains of Kōya?

[T.N. The poem is a warning: by tradition this stream was poisoned by insects, and the poem tells travellers not to drink.]

**Settsu: the Jewel River**
> Even the wind in the pines sounds lonely enough in autumn —
> and now, in the village by the Jewel River,
> they are beating cloth.

**Musashi: the Jewel River**
> Like the hand-woven cloth they bleach in the Jewel River,
> over and over, why is it
> I long so for someone of long ago?

[T.N. "Over and over" (さら〳〵) puns on the bleaching (さらす) of the cloth; 調布 (*tazukuri*, hand-woven cloth) also names the district.]

**Mutsu: the Jewel River of Noda**
> When evening comes, across the sea wind,
> at the Jewel River of Noda in the far north,
> the plovers are crying.

**Ōmi: the Jewel River of Noji**
> I will come back to seek it — the Jewel River of Noji,
> where bush clover grows, and on its coloured waves
> the moon [comes to] lodge.

[T.N. The end of this poem is worn on the scan ([…] in the transcription); the last line follows the poem's usual sense, the moon reflected on waves tinted by the clover.]
